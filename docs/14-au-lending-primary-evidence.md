# Australian business lending — the primary evidence, and what it does to the pitch

20 Aug 2026. Deep-research round 2. This fills the gap flagged as most important in docs 11 and
13: primary-source Australian data on the buyer-lender iteration loop. Researched directly
against RBA and ASBFEO sources after the assigned agent failed to report twice.

**Read the counter-evidence in Part 3 before using any of Part 1 in a pitch.** The most recent
RBA work says the thing we are selling has been getting better without us. That is survivable,
but only if we are precise about which segment we serve.

---

## Part 1 — The RBA states our thesis in its own words

The strongest finding in this entire research programme. RBA *Bulletin*, September 2018,
"Access to Small Business Finance" (Connolly and Bank), reporting what entrepreneurs on the
Bank's own Small Business Finance Advisory Panel told it. The heading is theirs:

> ### "The process to obtain finance is lengthy and onerous"
>
> "Entrepreneurs report that **banks are reluctant to provide them with advice on how to obtain
> finance.** As a result, they find it difficult to compare lending products and determine the one
> that would suit them best. When they apply, they **have to provide a large amount of information
> and documentation.** The banks then **take a long time to decide** whether to extend the loan.
> **This can be particularly problematic if the business opportunity is time sensitive.**"

Four sentences, and all four legs of the completeness arbitrage:

| Our claim | The RBA's words |
|---|---|
| The requirement set is tacit | "banks are reluctant to provide them with advice on how to obtain finance" |
| Lender-appetite routing has value | "difficult to compare lending products and determine the one that would suit them best" |
| The documentation burden is the cost | "a large amount of information and documentation" |
| The loop costs calendar time | "take a long time to decide" |
| **Option decay** | "particularly problematic if the business opportunity is time sensitive" |

This is a central bank, reporting first-hand from business owners, describing the exact mechanic.
It is a far better citation than any broker blog, and it should replace them in the pitch deck.

Two supporting findings from the same article:

**The tacit standard is explicitly acknowledged.** "Many small businesses are unaware of factors
that can affect lenders' assessments of their creditworthiness" — and the RBA's suggested remedy
is disclosure: **"More targeted information could also be provided by lenders when they do not
approve loan applications."** That is the silent-failure mechanic, identified by the RBA, with
"tell applicants why they failed" proposed as the fix. Eight years on, that has not happened.

**Evidence-readiness is a known constraint.** Citing ASBFEO, "up to **45 per cent** of small
businesses do not use accountancy software to maintain up-to-date and accurate financial
records", and market participants noted that with better accounting processes "these businesses
could more easily produce evidence of their financial performance when they apply for a loan."

**And an unexpected corroboration of the practitioner voice.** Doc 12 quotes a US practitioner
whose deal died over a discharged-but-unreleased UCC filing that "wasn't even our debt". The RBA
documents the identical failure in the Australian PPSR: security interests "are often not removed
when the borrower has fully repaid the loan", the owner must apply to have them removed, and
"removing an entry on the PPSR can be a lengthy process, which can then delay access to secured
finance for the business." Scale: **7 million business-related PPSR registrations against 2.2
million actively trading businesses, including 2.6 million pre-2012 interests still current, some
relating to loans already repaid.**

An anonymous Reddit anecdote and an RBA Bulletin article describing the same failure mode is
about as good as triangulation gets in this project.

## Part 2 — The funnel number, and the reason nobody shops

ASBFEO, *Affordable Capital for SME Growth* (June 2018), reporting a MoneyQuest SME survey run
1 July 2017 – 20 June 2018:

- Most enquiries **do not go further than the initial conversation.**
- 54% of enquiries are referred; of those referred, 41% are approved.
- **Therefore fewer than 22.5% of enquiries result in an approved referral.**

And ASBFEO's diagnosis, which is our thesis almost verbatim:

> "The rate of rejection **before application** reflects banks' move to automated processes **using
> check lists** to assess the initial enquiry. As SMEs typically have less documentation and
> shorter financial histories than large corporations, **they often fail at this first hurdle.**"

Failure at a checklist applied before a formal application even exists. The report also cites a
Jobs for NSW study finding SMEs are **four times more likely to be rejected for lending than
large businesses.**

Then the finding with the sharpest commercial implication:

> "Banks tell us that **only one in a hundred business owners over 30 years of age would consider
> changing banks.** This manifests as a presumption by SMEs that **when their bank declines a loan,
> they will likely have the same experience elsewhere.**"

Set that against doc 12's practitioner observation — *"I've had many lenders reject a deal just
for another lender to approve it"* — and you get a clean arbitrage. **The same file passes at one
bank and fails at another, and essentially nobody shops, because a decline is read as a verdict
on the business rather than on the lender's appetite.** Lender-appetite routing is therefore worth
real money, and it is a product the borrower cannot build and the incumbent bank will never sell.

**Caveats, stated plainly.** Both reports are 2018. The 22.5% figure is *enquiry*-level, not
application-level, and comes from a broker (MoneyQuest) via ASBFEO — a secondary source inside a
primary report. Use it as a directional funnel, not a statistic. Note also that our earlier
citation of "1 in 3 SMEs declined or partially funded (ASBFEO 2023)" traces to content marketing,
not to ASBFEO; the ASBFEO inquiry is 2018. **Correct that attribution.**

## Part 3 — The counter-evidence, which is serious

RBA *Bulletin*, October 2025, "Small Business Economic and Financial Conditions" (Harvey, Lai and
Spiller) — the current picture, and it is not the 2018 one.

Still supportive of us:

- **One in five SMEs** has experienced challenges obtaining finance (Banjo Loans SME Compass 2025).
- The most commonly reported challenges include **"long processing times"** alongside strict
  lender requirements, rate, and collateral demands.
- **"Fast decision times are important for many small businesses, especially those that need
  funding quickly to take advantage of growth opportunities."** Option decay, in the RBA's words,
  in 2025.
- Unsecured credit remains **below 5%** of SME credit. New loans secured with residential property
  are on average **four-and-a-half times as large** as non-residentially secured loans — so a
  larger acquisition loan still tends to mean pledging the family home.

But then, directly against us:

> "Access to finance for SMEs has improved along numerous dimensions over the past couple of
> years. These include more competitive pricing, **faster approval times, more streamlined
> application processes** and a broader range of funding products."

> "Many lenders have invested in digitisation and automation over the past few years to improve
> processing times, simplify application processes and reduce costs. This includes making use of
> customers' transaction histories and bank statement analysis to automate loan decision-making.
> **Numerous lenders have also simplified the loan application process by relaxing their
> documentation requirements for smaller SME loans.**"

Plus lenders "increasing the share of new SME loans that are granted through **automated approval
processes**".

This is the doc-11 counter-thesis arriving in our own market: **the gatekeeper is closing the
arbitrage itself.** Same pattern as eSTAR at the FDA, free draft notifications at the ACCC, the
FIRB portal checklist. Not a hypothetical risk — it is in the current RBA Bulletin.

### Why this does not kill the thesis, and the discipline it imposes

Read the sentence precisely: documentation requirements are being relaxed **for smaller SME
loans**, and automation runs on **transaction histories and bank statement analysis**. That works
where the question is "does this trading business service a modest facility". It does not work for
an acquisition:

- There is **no trading history under the new owner** — the transaction data being automated
  against does not exist for the entity that will hold the debt.
- The purchase price is mostly **goodwill**, which no bank statement predicts and no automated
  model will lend against unsecured.
- **Add-backs must be provable** (doc 12), which is a judgement about evidence, not a data feed.
- The buyer's **management equity** — can they run it — is a narrative assessment.
- Deal documents (LOI, lease assignment, vendor statement, PPSR releases) are not in any feed.

So the automation wave hollows out the **small, simple, self-serve end** and leaves the
**large, judgement-heavy, evidence-driven end** exactly where it was. Acquisition finance sits in
the second category. That is a defensible position — but it is a *narrower* claim than "business
lending is broken", and we should stop making the broad version.

**The honest formulation:** the parts of business lending that could be automated are being
automated. Acquisition lending cannot be, because the evidence a credit team needs does not exist
in any data feed — it has to be assembled, normalised and argued. That is the residual, and it is
where we operate.

## Part 4 — What still does not exist

**No primary Australian source distinguishes declines caused by documentation or process from
declines caused by credit merit.** Not the RBA, not ASBFEO, not the Productivity Commission, not
APRA, not the ACCC's small business loans work.

That absence is consistent with doc 13's measurement gap: every institution measures its own
speed; none measures the applicant's rework. It has two consequences:

1. **We cannot evidence the core claim with an Australian number.** The strongest available
   support is qualitative (the RBA's four sentences) plus a 2018 broker funnel and a US
   practitioner's estimate that half of a 90-day SBA close is information supply. Say so rather
   than dressing up content marketing as data.
2. **It is a moat.** If nobody measures the loop, no incumbent has a business case to fix it — and
   the first party to instrument it owns a number nobody else has. **Our own funnel becomes the
   dataset**: every requirement requested, every gap, every decline reason, every time-to-decision.
   That is an argument for instrumenting the pipeline from M0, and it is a D1 data-model decision.

Also unresolved: bank cost-to-assess a business loan file (no published Australian figure found),
and how many Australian business sales collapse on finance. Both are with the round-2 loop-
economics agent.

## Part 5 — Revised pitch numbers

Replace the content-marketing figures with these, in this order.

1. **The RBA quote.** "Banks are reluctant to provide them with advice on how to obtain finance…
   a large amount of information and documentation… take a long time to decide… particularly
   problematic if the business opportunity is time sensitive." — RBA *Bulletin*, Sep 2018. This is
   the whole pitch in one citation from an unimpeachable source.
2. **Fewer than 22.5% of SME finance enquiries result in an approved referral**, with rejection
   occurring *before application* against automated checklists — ASBFEO 2018 (MoneyQuest survey;
   directional).
3. **One in five SMEs reports difficulty obtaining finance, with "long processing times" among the
   top complaints** — RBA *Bulletin*, Oct 2025 (Banjo Loans survey).
4. **Only 1 in 100 business owners over 30 would consider changing banks**, so a decline is
   treated as universal — ASBFEO 2018. The case for appetite routing.
5. **SMEs are 4x more likely to be rejected for lending than large businesses** — Jobs for NSW
   2017, via ASBFEO.
6. **Unsecured credit is under 5% of SME lending; residentially secured loans average 4.5x
   larger** — RBA Oct 2025. Why an acquisition buyer ends up pledging the house.
7. **45% of small businesses don't use accounting software** — ASBFEO via RBA 2018. Why the
   evidence has to be assembled rather than exported.

And retire: the Bizcap 61%, the 43-days-versus-a-week settlement comparison, the "wrong lender,
wrong time, wrong documentation" line, and the "ASBFEO 2023" attribution. All content marketing.
The RBA says the same things with a citation that survives scrutiny.

## Addendum — two figures that appear not to exist publicly

Added 20 Aug 2026 after direct searching (the assigned agent signalled idle three times without
reporting; the fallback search backend is weak, so treat these as "not found" rather than
"definitively absent").

**1. Bank cost-to-assess an Australian business loan application.** No published Australian figure
found — not in RBA or APRA material, not in the big-four or Judo results packs surfaced, not in
consulting reports. The only relevant statements are qualitative and offshore, to the effect that
manual underwriting of small-ticket business loans is expensive relative to the revenue they
generate and the economics only work at scale. This matters because "here is what the loop costs
the bank" is the natural pitch to a lender, and we cannot currently source it. **Ask the bank
directly** — a credit team can answer "how many analyst hours does a declined acquisition file
consume" in one conversation, and that number would be worth more than anything published.

**2. Proportion of Australian business sales that fall over, and how often finance is the cause.**
No public figure found. Notable, though: the **AIBB operates BizStats, described as Australia's
largest database of completed business sales**, used by members for valuation and appraisal. It is
members-only.

That second point is worth flagging as an opportunity rather than a gap. A database of *completed*
Australian business sales is precisely the training data a ripeness or valuation model wants, and
it sits with an industry body whose members are business brokers — the people who feel the finance-
failure loop most directly. **AIBB is a potential data partner and distribution channel, not just
a source.** Worth raising with Mark alongside the Firmable question.

Both absences reinforce the doc-13 measurement gap: the loop is unmeasured on every side of the
transaction, by the banks, the brokers and the regulators alike.
